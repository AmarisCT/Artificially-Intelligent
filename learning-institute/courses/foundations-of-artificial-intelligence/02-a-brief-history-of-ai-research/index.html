A brief history of AI research

Today's systems are the latest stage of a seventy-year research effort with several competing approaches. Knowing the history helps you see which ideas are new and which are old.

Learning objectives

  • Place four milestones in order.
  • Contrast early rule-based approaches with learning-based approaches.
  • Explain why the history does not show a steady march towards human-like minds.

The question and the name

In 1950 Alan Turing published 'Computing Machinery and Intelligence', which argued that the question whether machines can think should be approached through behaviour [1]. In 1955 a proposal for a summer research project at Dartmouth College, written by John McCarthy and colleagues, used the phrase 'artificial intelligence' and proposed studying how machines could simulate aspects of learning and intelligence [2]. That proposal is often treated as the naming moment of the field.

Rules and learning

Early work often tried to write down knowledge and reasoning as explicit rules that a program could follow. Other researchers pursued systems that adjust themselves from examples. A key step for the learning tradition was a method for training networks with several layers, popularised in a 1986 paper on learning by back-propagating errors [3]. Historians describe periods when enthusiasm and funding for AI fell, followed by renewals when new methods or more computing power made new results possible.

In 2015 a review in the journal Nature described how deep learning, which trains many-layered networks on large data sets, had come to dominate tasks such as image and speech recognition [4]. In 2017 a paper introduced the transformer architecture, which processes sequences using a mechanism called attention [5]. In 2020 researchers reported that a very large language model could perform many tasks from a few examples given in its input [6].

Reading the history carefully

It is tempting to read this as a straight line towards human-like minds. The record does not support that reading. Different approaches succeeded at different tasks, and progress on one often did not carry over to others. Whether recent systems are on a path to general intelligence is an open question, not a conclusion of the history.

A worked example: two ways to build a spam filter

A rule-based filter might say: if the message contains these words, mark it as spam. A person writes and maintains the rules, and spammers can adapt around them. A learning-based filter is shown many messages labelled spam and not spam, and adjusts itself so that it predicts the labels. It may pick up patterns no one wrote down, and it needs new data as spam changes. Both are useful, and the history of AI contains both approaches, sometimes combined. The example shows why the shift from rules to learning mattered: it moved effort from writing knowledge to collecting and checking data.

Common misunderstandings

  • “AI research went in a straight line to today's systems.” Different approaches succeeded at different tasks and periods. Several ideas were revived after being set aside.
  • “The 1955 proposal predicted today's systems.” The proposal set out a research programme. Whether and when its goals would be reached was uncertain then and remains a matter of debate for general intelligence.

Reflection question

Which part of this history surprised you most, and why does it matter for how you read claims about AI today?
