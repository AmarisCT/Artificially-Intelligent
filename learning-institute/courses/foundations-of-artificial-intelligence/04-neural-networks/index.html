Neural networks

Neural networks are the basis of most recent progress in AI. They are loosely inspired by the brain but are mathematical models, not artificial brains. This lesson explains the idea and lets you train a very small one.

Learning objectives

  • Describe a neural network in terms of units, weights and layers.
  • Explain in outline how a network learns from its mistakes.
  • Say why a network with no hidden layer cannot learn some patterns.

Units, weights and layers

An artificial neural network consists of simple units that each take numbers in, combine them using adjustable weights, and pass a result on. Units are arranged in layers. Information flows from an input layer, through one or more hidden layers, to an output. 'Neural' reflects early inspiration from neurons, but real neurons are far more complex, and these networks should not be read as models of the brain's detailed workings.

The behaviour of a network is stored in its weights. Training means adjusting them. The 1986 paper on back-propagation described a way to calculate how each weight contributed to an error so that all of them could be adjusted a little in the direction that reduces it [1]. Repeating this over many examples gradually shapes the network.

Why layers matter

A single unit can separate inputs only along a straight boundary. Some simple patterns cannot be separated that way. The classic example is 'exclusive or' (XOR): output 1 when exactly one of two inputs is 1. A network that adds a hidden layer can represent it. The demonstration below lets you try each case. The point is general: depth gives networks the ability to build more complex features from simpler ones, which is a reason deep networks are effective on images and language [2].

What a network is not

A trained network does not contain explicit rules that a person can read off. Its competence is spread across many weights. This is one reason explaining why a network produced a particular output is difficult, a point the final lessons return to.

A worked example: why the network needs hidden units

Think of four inputs: (0,0), (0,1), (1,0), (1,1). For XOR, the answers are 0, 1, 1, 0. A single unit makes one straight cut across this space, with the input points on either side. No single straight line separates (0,1) and (1,0) from (0,0) and (1,1). A network with hidden units can make several cuts and combine them. That is the small-scale version of a general idea: layers let a network build complex distinctions from simple ones. It is also why bigger and deeper networks can capture more patterns, with the caution that more capacity is not the same as better understanding.

Common misunderstandings

  • “Neural networks work like human brains.” They were loosely inspired by neurons, but they are mathematical models and differ from brains in many ways.
  • “A trained network contains rules a person can read.” Its behaviour is spread across many weights. Explaining a particular output can be hard.

Reflection question

Why might calling artificial neural networks 'brain-like' be misleading? Give one similarity and one difference.
