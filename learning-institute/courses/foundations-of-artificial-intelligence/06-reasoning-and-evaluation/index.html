Lesson 6 of 8 · about 10 minutes

Reasoning and evaluation

How do we know what an AI system can do? This lesson looks at benchmarks and tests, what they can and cannot show, and the debate about what counts as understanding.

Learning objectives

  • Explain what a benchmark is and what it measures.
  • Name two reasons a high score may not show what it appears to.
  • Summarise the disagreement about whether language models understand.

Tests as evidence

A benchmark is a standard set of tasks with agreed scoring, used to compare systems. Benchmarks make progress measurable, but a score is only as informative as the match between the test and the ability it is meant to reflect. A system might score well because it learned shortcuts, because similar questions appeared in its training data, or because the test measures a narrower skill than its name suggests.

Reasoning and generalisation

People often ask whether a model 'reasons'. A careful answer begins by saying what is being measured: solving a certain kind of problem, producing a correct final answer, producing a valid step-by-step argument, or handling new problem types. These are different. A system might do well on one and poorly on another. Turing's imitation game is a behavioural test that tries to bypass these distinctions, and critics have argued that behavioural tests can be passed by systems that lack what we care about [1, 2].

A live debate

Researchers who have surveyed the debate over whether large language models understand language describe two broad camps: those who think that such systems may have some form of understanding, and those who think that statistical patterns in text cannot amount to it. They note that the debate is hampered by the lack of an agreed account of understanding and of tests that would separate the views [3]. This is a pre-print summary of the debate, not a verdict. The lesson's aim is to show how to hold such a debate open.

A worked example: two systems, the same score

Suppose two systems each score 80% on a reading comprehension test. One does so by understanding the passages. The other has learned that the answer is usually in the first sentence, a pattern in this test's design. Their scores are identical. They would behave very differently on a test where the answer is elsewhere. The example shows why researchers build varied tests and look for failure cases, and why a single number can mislead. It also shows why 'understanding' is hard to define through tests alone: different internal routes can produce the same result.

Common misunderstandings

  • “A high benchmark score means a system has the human ability the benchmark is named after.” Names can overstate what is measured, and systems can exploit shortcuts or overlap with training data.
  • “The debate about understanding is only semantic.” The terms matter, but so does evidence about how systems behave on new problems and what they represent internally. Researchers disagree about both.

Reflection question

Design a test for 'understanding' of a short story by a person or a machine. What would a passing performance show, and what might it fail to show?

Open question

Is behavioral imitation sufficient evidence of understanding? The Unanswered Archive keeps this question open.
