Intelligence versus consciousness

A system might be very capable and still not be conscious, or the reverse. This lesson explains why the two questions should be kept apart, and what current research says about connecting them.

Learning objectives

  • Explain why intelligence and consciousness are different questions.
  • Summarise two views on whether computation could suffice for consciousness.
  • Describe how scientific theories are being used to assess AI systems.

Two questions

'What can this system do?' is a question about intelligence or capability. 'Does it feel like anything to be this system?' is a question about consciousness. Capability can be measured by tasks. Experience cannot be read off from task performance in the same way. The Unanswered Archive asks whether intelligence can exist without consciousness, and treats it as open.

Does computation suffice?

The philosopher John Searle argued with his Chinese Room thought experiment that running a program is not enough for understanding: a person following rules to manipulate Chinese symbols could produce good answers without understanding Chinese [1]. Many replies have been offered, and the argument remains debated [2]. Others hold that if the right kind of information processing is what matters for consciousness, then machines could in principle be conscious. Which view is right depends on which theory of consciousness is right, and that is unsettled.

Using science to assess AI

Because theories of consciousness make different claims about what matters, a research group proposed assessing AI systems against indicator properties drawn from several theories, and reported that no current system was a strong candidate [3]. Dehaene and colleagues likewise distinguished kinds of information processing associated with consciousness in the brain and asked which might be implemented in machines [4]. Chalmers examined the question for large language models and weighed considerations for and against [5]. These works are proposals and arguments, several are preprints, and none shows that any system is or is not conscious.

A worked example: capability without a verdict

A chess program beats grandmasters. It is, by one definition, highly capable at chess. Does that tell us anything about whether it feels anything while playing? Most people think not. Now consider a conversational system that talks about its feelings. Its outputs are closer to the way a person would describe experience, and for that reason more tempting to take at face value. But a system trained on a great deal of human text may produce such language whatever is going on inside. To get evidence you would need something other than fluency: a theory that links particular properties to experience, and checks of whether the system has those properties. That is what the indicator-property work attempts, with the limits described in the lesson.

Common misunderstandings

  • “Intelligent systems must be conscious.” Capability and experience are different questions. The Archive treats whether intelligence can exist without consciousness as open.
  • “Searle proved that machines cannot think.” The Chinese Room argument is a contested argument about whether running a program suffices for understanding. It has attracted many replies.

Reflection question

Give an example of a system that is very capable at a task. What additional evidence, beyond task performance, would you need before asking whether it is conscious?

Open question

Can intelligence exist without consciousness? The Unanswered Archive keeps this question open.
