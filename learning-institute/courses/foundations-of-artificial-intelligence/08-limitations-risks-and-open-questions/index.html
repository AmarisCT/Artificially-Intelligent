Lesson 8 of 8 · about 10 minutes

Limitations, risks, and open questions

This lesson closes the course by looking at what AI systems get wrong, how people and institutions try to manage the risks, and which questions remain open.

Learning objectives

  • Name three kinds of limitation or risk of AI systems.
  • Describe what a risk management framework is for.
  • List open questions the course has raised.

Where systems fall short

AI systems can be inaccurate, can perform worse for groups under-represented in their data, can reveal or misuse personal information, and can be hard to explain. They can also be used in ways their builders did not intend. Critics of large language models have raised concerns about training on unvetted data, about social harms, and about the costs of scale [1]. Planting convincing but false memories in people is a documented phenomenon in human memory research [2], which is one reason to be careful when fluent systems generate plausible but unreliable content.

Managing risk

A risk management framework is a structured way to identify, assess and reduce harms. The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology published an AI Risk Management Framework for organisations that design, develop, deploy or use AI systems; it is voluntary, and it organises the work into functions for governing, mapping, measuring and managing risk [3]. Frameworks like this do not make a system safe by themselves, but they give organisations a shared vocabulary and checklist.

What remains open

Whether systems can understand meaning, whether reasoning in machines is comparable to human reasoning, whether any artificial system could be conscious, and under what conditions an artificial system could deserve moral consideration are open questions that the Unanswered Archive tracks. A good student of AI can describe the state of the evidence on each without claiming more than it supports.

A worked example: a hiring tool

Imagine a tool that ranks job applicants, trained on a company's past hiring decisions. If past decisions favoured certain groups, a model trained to imitate them can reproduce that pattern even though no one told it to. Checking requires deciding what fair means (equal selection rates, equal accuracy, something else), measuring it on the relevant groups, and checking again over time. It also requires deciding who is responsible if the tool causes harm. Risk management frameworks encourage organisations to ask such questions before and after deployment. The example shows that many risks come from the data and the setting, not only the algorithm.

Common misunderstandings

  • “A machine cannot be biased.” Systems learn from data produced by people and can reproduce its gaps and biases.
  • “A risk management framework guarantees safety.” It structures the work of identifying and reducing risk. It does not make a system safe by itself.

Reflection question

Choose one AI application you encounter. Who could be harmed if it fails, and what would you want its builders to have checked?

Open question

How should responsibility be assigned when AI systems participate in consequential decisions? The Unanswered Archive keeps this question open.
