What artificial intelligence means

The phrase artificial intelligence is used for a research field, for a family of technologies, and, in popular writing, for almost any clever software. This lesson sorts out what the term does and does not cover.

Learning objectives

  • Give two different meanings of 'artificial intelligence'.
  • Describe how different definitions of intelligence lead to different tests.
  • Explain why 'AI' is not one kind of technology.

A field and a set of systems

Artificial intelligence names both a research programme, the attempt to build machines that act intelligently, and the systems that programme produces. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on the subject treats it as a field with several traditions and goals rather than as a single method [1]. That already tells you something: an article that says 'AI did this' is rarely specific enough to know what kind of system is meant.

An email spam filter, a chess engine, a route planner, a system that recognises faces, and a chatbot are all called AI. They work in different ways, were built with different methods, and fail in different ways.

What counts as intelligence?

Intelligence has no single agreed definition, and the choice of definition shapes what counts as success. Alan Turing proposed replacing the question 'can machines think?' with a behavioural test, an imitation game in which an interrogator tries to tell a machine from a person by conversation [2]. Other researchers have tried to define intelligence formally, for example as an agent's ability to achieve goals across a wide range of environments [3]. A behavioural test and a formal definition ask different things.

The point is not to pick a winner. It is to see that when someone says a system 'is intelligent', the claim depends on a definition, and that definition may differ from the one you hold.

Narrow and general

Most deployed AI systems are built for particular tasks and perform them well within a limited range. Researchers often distinguish such task-specific systems from the hoped-for goal of more general capability. Whether current systems are moving towards generality, and how to tell, is debated. This course treats those questions as open.

A worked example: three headlines

Consider three headlines: 'AI detects cancer', 'AI writes poetry' and 'AI beats world champion at Go'. In each, 'AI' stands for a different system, built differently, trained on different data, and evaluated differently. The first might be an image classifier trained on labelled scans, the second a language model, the third a system trained by playing games against itself. A result in one of these says little directly about the others. A reader who asks 'which system, what task, how was it tested?' will usually learn more from a story than one who treats 'AI' as one thing.

Common misunderstandings

  • “AI is a single technology.” It is a field with several methods. Rule-based systems, statistical learning and neural networks work differently.
  • “If a system is called intelligent, it can do many things.” Most systems are built for narrow tasks. A system that is excellent at one task may be unable to do a neighbouring one.

Reflection question

Write your own working definition of intelligence in one or two sentences. Then name one system that your definition would count as intelligent and one it would not.

Open question

Can machine reasoning become functionally comparable to human reasoning? The Unanswered Archive keeps this question open.
