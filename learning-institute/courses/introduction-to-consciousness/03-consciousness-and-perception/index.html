Consciousness and perception

Perception feels like looking through a window. Evidence from psychology and neuroscience suggests it is closer to an active construction. This lesson also introduces the main experimental trick for studying consciousness: holding the stimulus fixed and varying what is experienced.

Learning objectives

  • Explain why perception is more than receiving signals.
  • Describe the 'contrastive' method for studying conscious perception.
  • Distinguish processing a stimulus from consciously perceiving it.

Perception is not a window

Perception is the process by which the brain turns signals from the senses into an interpretation of the world. The signals are incomplete and noisy, and the brain fills in gaps using what it expects. One influential family of ideas, predictive processing, describes perception as the brain continually predicting its input and updating the predictions when they are wrong [1]. Whether the framework is right in detail is debated. The general lesson that perception depends on interpretation is widely accepted.

This matters for consciousness because what we are aware of is already an interpretation. When you see a stable, colourful world, you are not seeing raw data.

Same stimulus, different experience

To study consciousness scientifically, researchers want to change what a person experiences without changing what is physically present. In some set-ups a faint image is shown for a very short time so that it is sometimes reported and sometimes not. In others, two different images are shown, one to each eye, and perception tends to alternate between them even though the input stays the same. Comparing brain activity when the same stimulus is reported with when it is not is called the contrastive method.

The method is powerful, but its logic is subtle. The brain activity that differs between 'seen' and 'not seen' trials might be the neural basis of experience. It might also reflect the consequences of seeing, such as getting ready to report. Separating these is a central methodological problem in the field.

Processing without awareness

Studies reviewed by researchers such as Stanislas Dehaene suggest that some stimuli can be processed and can influence behaviour without being reported as seen [2]. If so, perception and conscious perception are not identical, and a system that processes information is not thereby shown to be conscious. This idea will matter again when we ask about machines.

A worked example: the blind spot

Each eye has a small region where the optic nerve leaves the retina and there are no light-sensitive cells. You do not see a hole in your visual field, because the brain fills in the gap using the surroundings. You can notice it with a simple demonstration: close one eye, fix your gaze on a mark, and slowly move a small object across the side of your view until it vanishes. The example shows that what you experience is not a direct copy of the input. It also illustrates why researchers separate the stimulus from the experience: the same missing input is present at all times, yet nothing is experienced as missing.

Common misunderstandings

  • “If a brain region responds to a stimulus, the person is conscious of it.” Studies reviewed in this lesson suggest that stimuli can be processed and can influence behaviour without being reported as seen.
  • “Perception is a passive recording of the world.” Perception relies on interpretation and expectation as well as signals from the senses. Illusions and filling-in show this.

Reflection question

Describe an optical illusion you know. What does it show about the difference between what is in the world and what you experience?
