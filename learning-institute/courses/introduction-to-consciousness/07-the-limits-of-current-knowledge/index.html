Introduction to Consciousness / Lesson 7

Lesson 7 of 8 · about 10 minutes

The limits of current knowledge

A good student of any field learns where its knowledge ends. This lesson maps what is not known about consciousness, and how that bears on animals and artificial systems.

Learning objectives

  • Name three questions that remain unresolved.
  • Explain why measuring consciousness in other beings is difficult.
  • Describe how uncertainty about theories affects claims about machines.

What is not known

Researchers do not agree on what makes an experience conscious, whether consciousness can be measured objectively, or which neural processes are necessary for it. The Unanswered Archive records each of these as open questions with the problems that remain. A clear sense of what is unknown is part of understanding the field, not a failure of it.

Other minds and other animals

We rely on behaviour and similarity to judge whether other beings have experiences. Most people infer that other humans do, because they are similar to us and can say so. For animals, reviews of the question consider behaviour, brain structure and evolutionary relationships, and reach graded rather than certain conclusions [1]. The difficulty is not carelessness. It is that experience is not directly observable from outside.

Machines

The same difficulty applies to artificial systems, and with an added problem: a system trained on human language can produce fluent statements about experience without that being evidence for it. A group of researchers proposed assessing AI systems by checking them against indicator properties drawn from leading scientific theories, and concluded that no current system was a strong candidate, while noting no obvious barriers to building systems that satisfy more of the indicators [2]. That report is a preprint and its approach depends on the theories it draws on. Another paper by the philosopher David Chalmers examines whether large language models could be conscious and weighs considerations on both sides [3], also a preprint.

The sensible conclusion is not 'yes' or 'no'. It is that claims about machine consciousness are only as strong as the theories and evidence behind them, and those are unsettled.

A worked example: the octopus and the thermostat

People disagree about whether an octopus has experiences, and few people think a thermostat does. What separates those cases? Some answers appeal to behaviour (the octopus learns, explores and responds flexibly), some to biology (a nervous system with many neurons), and some to theory (a view about what kinds of processing matter). Each answer depends on assumptions that are themselves unsettled. If a theory says what matters is the right kind of widespread information sharing, then the answer might depend on a system's architecture. If a theory says it depends on physical structure, a different answer follows. The example shows how a question about other beings is linked to the question about theory, and why the reasoning has to be explicit.

Common misunderstandings

  • “If a machine says it is conscious, we should believe it.” Fluent self-description can be produced by systems trained on human language whether or not there is experience. It is not strong evidence on its own.
  • “If we do not know whether machines are conscious, the answer must be no.” Not knowing is different from a negative answer. A careful position states what the evidence does and does not support.

Reflection question

What kind of evidence would you need before you were willing to say a non-human system has experiences? Why that evidence?

Open question

What would count as evidence of machine consciousness? The Unanswered Archive keeps this question open.
