Introduction to Consciousness / Lesson 6

Lesson 6 of 8 · about 11 minutes

Major scientific and philosophical theories

There is no single accepted theory of consciousness. This lesson introduces six that appear in the Theory Chamber, what each is trying to explain, and why listing a theory is not the same as endorsing it.

Learning objectives

  • State in one sentence the core idea of at least four theories.
  • Explain how theories differ in what they treat as the key ingredient.
  • Describe why the existence of many theories reflects an unsettled field.

Why there are many theories

A recent review set out to survey the main scientific approaches side by side and found that they differ in what they take consciousness to depend on, in what evidence they emphasise, and in how they would be tested [1]. The Theory Chamber follows the same idea: it lays out claims, supporting sources and criticisms for each theory without ranking them. Being listed there means a theory is a serious subject of study. It does not mean it has been shown to be correct.

Six ideas, in brief

Global Neuronal Workspace Theory proposes that a representation becomes consciously accessible when it is amplified and made available to many specialised processes through a distributed network [2].

Integrated Information Theory starts from properties of experience, such as its unity, and proposes that consciousness corresponds to the cause-effect structure of a physical system [3].

Higher-Order Theories hold that a mental state is conscious when the subject has a higher-order representation of being in that state [4].

Recurrent Processing Theory associates consciousness with feedback (recurrent) processing in sensory areas rather than with the first feedforward sweep [5].

Attention Schema Theory proposes that subjective awareness is the brain's simplified model of its own attention [6].

Predictive Processing approaches apply the idea of the brain as a prediction machine to consciousness, for instance as a basis for identifying its neural correlates [7].

Competition, not a ranking

Theories disagree about, for example, whether consciousness depends on widespread broadcast, on the structure of causal interactions, or on how the brain represents itself. These disagreements lead to different predictions, which is why adversarial tests are valuable. One such test of two of the theories reported that key claims of each were challenged, and the authors did not declare either theory proven or disproven [8]. A dispute over whether one of the theories should be labelled pseudoscience has also been reported; it shows that disagreement exists, and is not itself a finding [9].

A worked example: one finding, six readings

Imagine a result in which a late burst of widespread activity follows a stimulus that people report seeing, and does not follow one they miss. A workspace theory can read this as the stimulus being broadcast. A recurrent processing theory might say the important event is earlier feedback activity, and that the late burst reflects reporting. A higher-order theory might look for activity that represents the person's own state. A predictive processing account might ask how the activity relates to prediction error. The same observation can be placed in several frameworks. This is why theories are compared on what each predicts in new experiments, not on whether each can be made to fit a result after the fact.

Common misunderstandings

  • “The Theory Chamber ranks theories from best to worst.” It does not. It lays out each theory's claims, sources and criticisms so that you can compare them.
  • “If a theory is listed in the Chamber, it has been confirmed.” Listing a theory means it is a serious subject of research. It does not mean the theory is correct.

Reflection question

Pick one theory. What kind of evidence would make you more or less confident in it?
