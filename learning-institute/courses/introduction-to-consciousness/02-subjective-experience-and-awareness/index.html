Lesson 2 of 8 · about 11 minutes

Subjective experience and awareness

Why does consciousness seem so much harder to explain than memory or vision? This lesson introduces the first-person point of view and the puzzle often called the hard problem.

Learning objectives

  • Explain what 'subjective' means in this context.
  • Describe the difference between the 'easy' and 'hard' problems of consciousness.
  • Say why someone's report of an experience is not the same as the experience.

The first-person point of view

Some facts about you can be checked by anyone: your height, your heart rate, which words you said. Others seem to be available in full only to you: how the coffee tastes right now, what your headache feels like. Subjective experience is this first-person side of mental life.

Nagel used the example of a bat, which finds its way around by echolocation [1]. We can learn a great deal about how bat sonar works. Nagel's point was that knowing the mechanism does not obviously tell us what the bat's experience is like, if it has one. The example is meant to show a limit of describing minds only from the outside, not to settle what bats experience.

Easy problems and the hard problem

The philosopher David Chalmers separated problems that are 'easy' from one that is 'hard' [2]. The easy problems, in his sense, are about functions: how the brain discriminates stimuli, integrates information, focuses attention, or produces reports about its own states. They are far from easy in practice, but they are the kind of problem that scientific explanation of mechanisms seems well suited to. The hard problem is why any of this processing is accompanied by experience at all.

Not everyone accepts the framing. Some philosophers argue that once the functions are explained, nothing is left over to explain, and that the intuition of a further puzzle is misleading. Others think the puzzle is real. The Lexicon and the Unanswered Archive keep these positions separate rather than choosing one.

Report is not experience

Because experience is private, researchers usually rely on reports: a participant says whether they saw a flash. Reports are useful, but they are not the experience itself. A person might have an experience they cannot describe, or report one they did not have. A good deal of consciousness research is about how to separate what a person experienced from what they were able or willing to report, and the next lessons return to that problem.

A worked example: pain and its description

Imagine that you and a friend both stub your toes. You each say 'that hurt'. A brain scanner shows similar activity in each of you. Does this show that the two experiences feel the same? The scanner shows what is common in the physical process. The words show that you each use the same label. Neither directly shows how it feels to each of you. This is not to suggest that the experiences differ, only that the evidence available to an outsider is of a different kind from the experience itself. It is why researchers who study consciousness are careful about the difference between what a person reports and what a person experiences, and why the problem is described as hard even for something as ordinary as a stubbed toe.

Common misunderstandings

  • “The hard problem says science cannot study consciousness.” The hard problem is a claim about one kind of question, why processing is accompanied by experience. It does not say that studying mechanisms, such as how attention or report works, is useless. Much of the field does exactly that.
  • “Everyone agrees there is a hard problem.” They do not. Some philosophers think the puzzle disappears once the functions are explained. The lesson presents it as a live position, not a settled one.

Reflection question

Describe an experience that you find hard to put into words. What do you think is lost when you describe it to someone else?

Open question

Why does subjective experience exist? The Unanswered Archive keeps this question open.
