Neural correlates of consciousness

If experience depends on the brain, which parts and processes matter? This lesson introduces the search for neural correlates, and the care needed to say what a correlation shows.

Learning objectives

  • Define a neural correlate of consciousness.
  • Explain why correlation does not identify the basis of experience.
  • Name two limitations of studies that search for neural correlates.

What is a neural correlate?

A neural correlate of consciousness (NCC) is a pattern of brain activity that goes along with a particular conscious experience. The idea is often framed as the minimal set of neural events that is jointly sufficient for a specific experience [1]. Researchers look for correlates using methods that record or image brain activity, such as EEG and fMRI, while participants perceive or report experiences.

The term deliberately avoids saying that the activity produces experience. It names a pattern that accompanies it, leaving open how they are related.

Correlation is only the first step

Finding that activity A tracks experience B does not show that A is what makes B conscious. A might be a prerequisite that enables experience, such as a general state of arousal. It might be a consequence, such as the brain preparing to report. Or it might be involved in both seeing and not seeing. Review articles on the field emphasise distinguishing these cases [1].

Another difficulty is that different theories read the same data differently. Two theories can agree that a region is active and still disagree about why it matters. This is part of why researchers have turned to adversarial collaborations, in which proponents of rival theories agree in advance on what results would count for or against each [2].

What the search has and has not shown

The search for NCC has produced a large body of findings about the brain activity that accompanies reported awareness. It has not produced agreement on what produces experience. A recent adversarial test of two leading theories reported results that partly matched predictions of both and substantially challenged key claims of each, without declaring either proven or disproven [3]. This is typical of an active field: methods are improving, and important questions remain open.

A worked example: reading a headline

Suppose a headline says 'Scientists find the seat of consciousness'. Ask what the study could have shown. It may have compared brain activity when people reported seeing a picture with activity when they did not. If a region differed, the careful description is that the region's activity is associated with reporting a picture. The next questions are whether the region is necessary (would removal or disruption abolish experience, and not just the report?), whether it is sufficient, and whether the result holds in other experiments and with other methods. A headline that moves from 'associated with' to 'is where consciousness lives' has added claims the study did not test.

Common misunderstandings

  • “A neural correlate is the cause of consciousness.” The term names an accompanying pattern. It leaves open whether the pattern produces, enables or follows experience.
  • “Brain scans can read whether someone is conscious.” They can show activity that tends to accompany reported experience. Interpreting a scan for an individual is a further step with its own limits.

Reflection question

Suppose a brain scan could tell you reliably whether someone is having an experience. What would still be unexplained?

Open question
