Introduction to Consciousness / Lesson 8

Lesson 8 of 8 · about 10 minutes

Final research reflection

This lesson asks you to use what you have learned to evaluate a claim and to plan a small investigation of your own.

Learning objectives

  • Apply a short checklist to a claim about consciousness.
  • Distinguish evidence, interpretation and speculation in a claim.
  • Pose a research question you could investigate further.

A checklist for any claim

Throughout this course the same few questions have helped. Which sense of consciousness is meant? Is the evidence about reports, about brain activity, or about behaviour? What would the claim predict, and what would count against it? Is the source a primary study, a review, a philosophical argument, or a news report? Is the claim a finding, an interpretation, or a speculation?

These questions do not tell you what is true. They help you notice how much weight a claim can bear.

Evidence, interpretation, speculation

Evidence is what was observed: that activity differed between conditions. Interpretation is what the authors say it means: that the region is part of the basis of awareness. Speculation goes further still: that therefore machines with a similar structure would be conscious. A strong reader keeps track of where in this chain a statement sits.

A worked example: applying the checklist

Take the sentence 'Brain imaging shows that dogs are conscious.' Which sense is meant? Probably experience. What was measured? Perhaps brain responses in dogs to familiar stimuli. Is that evidence about reports, activity or behaviour? Activity and behaviour, since dogs cannot report. What would the claim predict, and what would count against it? If experience goes with certain brain patterns in humans, similar patterns in dogs might suggest similar processes, though the link between those patterns and experience is itself under study. The source type matters too: a primary study, a review, or a news report. After working through this you may still think dogs are probably conscious, but you will be clear about which part of that belief rests on evidence and which on inference.

Common misunderstandings

  • “Having a method means the question is answered.” A method structures the thinking. It does not supply the evidence. The aim of the checklist is to see what a claim rests on.
  • “Admitting uncertainty is a weakness.” Stating what is unknown and what would settle it is part of responsible research.
