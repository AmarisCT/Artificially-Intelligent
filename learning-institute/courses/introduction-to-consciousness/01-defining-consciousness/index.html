Defining consciousness

Almost everyone uses the word consciousness, and almost no one means exactly the same thing by it. Before asking how consciousness works, this lesson separates the questions the word can hide.

Learning objectives

  • List four different things people mean by 'conscious'.
  • Explain the difference between wakefulness and experience.
  • Say what is at stake in separating phenomenal from access consciousness.

One word, several questions

Ask five people what consciousness is and you may get five answers: being awake, being aware of your surroundings, knowing yourself, having a stream of thoughts, or simply being someone to whom things happen. These are related, but they are not the same, and a good deal of confusion in public debate comes from sliding between them.

A useful first step is to sort the meanings. A patient under general anaesthesia is not conscious in the everyday sense of being awake. A person in a deep dreamless sleep is not either. But a person dreaming is unconscious of the room around them while still having vivid experiences. So being awake and having experiences can come apart.

Four meanings worth keeping apart

The first is wakefulness: the state of being alert and responsive, as opposed to being in deep sleep or a coma. The second is awareness of something in particular, such as noticing a sound or being aware that you are tired. The third is self-consciousness, which can mean awareness of yourself as a person, or simply embarrassment at being watched. The fourth is the sense most researchers have in mind when they say the subject is hard: that there is something it is like to be you, having an experience.

That fourth sense comes from the philosopher Thomas Nagel, who proposed that an organism has conscious mental states if there is something it is like to be that organism [1]. The phrase does not define consciousness in terms of anything else. It points to subjective experience and asks us to recognise it.

Two kinds of consciousness?

The philosopher Ned Block argued that two ideas are often run together [2]. Phenomenal consciousness is the felt, experiential side: how the red of a tomato looks. Access consciousness concerns information that is available for reasoning, report and the control of action. If these are really distinct, then a theory that explains how information becomes available to a whole system might not, by itself, explain why anything is felt. Other researchers dispute that they can be separated in this way; the distinction is itself part of the debate.

You do not need to settle this to learn from it. The point for now is practical: when you read a claim about consciousness, ask which sense is meant. A claim about wakefulness can be tested at a hospital bedside. A claim about phenomenal consciousness raises harder questions about what could count as evidence.

A worked example: the anaesthetic

Consider what is said about a patient during surgery. Doctors monitor how deeply the patient is anaesthetised, because the aim is that the patient is not awake and does not feel pain. In everyday speech the patient is 'unconscious'. Now suppose a rare case in which a patient cannot move or respond but later reports having heard voices during the operation. Whether such cases happen, and how often, is a medical question. What the example shows is that three questions are in play: was the patient responsive (a behavioural question), was the patient aware of anything (a question about access to information), and was there something it was like for the patient (a question about experience)? A single word covers all three, and an answer to one does not automatically answer the others.

Common misunderstandings

  • “Consciousness just means being awake.” Wakefulness is one sense. People can have experiences while asleep, as in dreams, and can be awake while attending to nothing in particular. Researchers who ask the hard questions usually mean experience.
  • “Philosophers and scientists mean the same thing by the word.” They often do not. Philosophy tends to ask what experience is, neuroscience asks which processes accompany it, and AI research asks whether the question applies to machines.

Reflection question

Think of a moment when you were awake but not aware of something that was right in front of you. Which of the senses from this lesson were present, and which were missing?
