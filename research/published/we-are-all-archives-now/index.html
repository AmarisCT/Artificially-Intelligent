We Are All Archives Now

Historical archives began with the needs of the living. Today, our messages, photographs, and digital traces create records that circulate beyond us. What changes when artificial intelligence learns from what we leave behind?

October 7, 2026

Long before a photograph could be stored in the cloud, a human being pressed a mark into clay. The material was different, but the impulse is familiar: make something endure beyond the moment in which it happened. A delivery, an obligation, a name, a story. Something needed to remain available after the people who witnessed it had moved on.

We tend to imagine archives as places where history is stored after it has finished happening. Yet many historical records began as instruments of ordinary life. They were made to manage resources, establish ownership, communicate decisions, or settle disputes. Their creators were not always trying to address the future. The future inherited what their present required.

Among the earliest surviving Mesopotamian tablets are economic records. The Metropolitan Museum of Art describes early tablets, dating to around 3300 BCE, that recorded economic information through pictographs and numerals. An account of barley was useful because someone needed to know what had been received or distributed. Centuries later, the same object could become evidence of how a society organized itself. Its purpose changed as its surroundings disappeared. [1]

This is one way a record becomes historical: it outlives the transaction that made it necessary. But survival alone does not explain an archive. Someone must also recover, identify, arrange, interpret, and preserve what remains. A fragment without context can endure physically while losing much of its meaning.

The seventh-century BCE Library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh offers another route into the past. Its surviving clay tablets and fragments bring together scholarly and literary traditions, including texts associated with divination, medicine, and the Epic of Gilgamesh. This was a royal collection shaped by particular interests and institutions, rather than a complete account of everyone living in the Assyrian world. The British Museum's ongoing research treats its organization and formation as questions to investigate. [2]

A library and an archive are not identical. A library can gather works from many origins; an archive generally preserves records in relation to the people or institutions that produced them. Their histories nevertheless intersect in a larger human activity: arranging traces so that knowledge can travel beyond its original setting.

That arrangement is never innocent of perspective. A collection tells us something about the people who created its contents, but also about those who decided to collect them. What survives may reveal an institution's priorities more clearly than the full experience of the society around it.

Domesday Book makes that relationship visible. Produced from the survey of 1086, it records landholding and resources in much of England. The National Archives describes it as a detailed survey and valuation of landed property. For a modern researcher, it is a major historical source. For the government that commissioned it, the information helped make property and obligations legible to authority. [3]

The record and the person are already separated here. A landholder can appear as an entry within a system, while the fears, attachments, and contradictions of that person's life remain outside its categories. The document may be accurate about what it was designed to measure and still be radically incomplete as a portrait of being human.

Historical archives take shape through this combination of purpose, survival, and selection. Some materials are deliberately retained. Others survive through accident. Still others disappear through destruction, neglect, or exclusion. Modern archival practice makes selection explicit: the UK's National Archives guides public bodies in identifying records for permanent preservation. Keeping everything is not the same as preserving a usable historical record. [4]

This matters because absence can be mistaken for insignificance. A person whose voice was not recorded did not live a less meaningful life. A community poorly represented in official documents did not lack a history. What an archive makes visible depends partly on who had access to writing, who possessed institutional authority, and which materials later received care. Reading history requires attention to those limits.

Today, the making of records has moved into the texture of ordinary activity. We send messages, upload photographs, save documents, and interact with systems that may log our actions. A personal collection grows beside us, distributed across devices and accounts. Other records are held by organizations whose purposes differ from our own.

These traces do not all belong to a single archive. Nor is every trace accessible to an AI system. Access depends on the service, its practices, permissions, and the material involved. Still, a change in scale is visible: an individual can now produce a volume of recorded expression that would once have required sustained institutional effort to create and maintain.

The Library of Congress treats personal digital archiving as a practical preservation problem, offering guidance for photographs, email, websites, and other personal materials. Its advice also complicates the promise that the internet never forgets. Digital content depends on technologies that keep it readable. Saving a file once does not guarantee that it will remain accessible. [5]

We therefore live with two different vulnerabilities. Information we wish would disappear may be copied beyond our control. Information we desperately want to keep may become inaccessible. Digital memory can be persistent in one setting and fragile in another. Neither total permanence nor effortless deletion describes it well.

The phrase 'we are all archives now' names this condition metaphorically. A person is not literally a repository. But increasingly, our lives acquire recorded counterparts that can be searched, rearranged, and interpreted apart from us. We are the creators of some records, the subjects of others, and only partial custodians of the whole.

Artificial intelligence introduces another layer into this history. Some language models are trained on large collections of recorded language. Brown and colleagues' 2020 GPT-3 paper, for example, describes a training mixture that included filtered Common Crawl material, WebText2, books, and Wikipedia. The model's ability to generate language emerged through training on this selected body of text, rather than through firsthand participation in the lives behind it. [6]

A trained model is not a conventional archive with a reliable catalogue of its sources. It learns statistical relationships in data, and it can sometimes reproduce particular passages, but its generated answers are not guaranteed retrievals of authentic records. A fluent response can contain invention. Treating it as historical evidence requires checking the underlying sources.

The philosophical connection is nevertheless significant. Human beings record their interpretations of the world. Machines learn patterns from some of those recordings. People then encounter machine-generated language and use it in further writing, decisions, and self-understanding. Expression becomes training material; generated expression becomes part of the next human environment. This is a feedback loop, not evidence that a collective consciousness has transferred into a machine.

The first essay in Artificially Intelligent asked whether human consciousness is connected to artificial intelligence. This essay approaches that question through what consciousness leaves behind. A letter can communicate grief without containing the experience of grieving. A photograph can preserve an appearance without preserving the person who inhabited it. A system that learns from human expression has encountered traces of thought, not demonstrated possession of the subjective lives that produced them.

That distinction becomes urgent when a digital representation is mistaken for a complete self. Records can describe someone at particular moments while fixing them in categories they have since outgrown. A profile assembled from old behavior may be useful for a system's purpose without being a fair account of the person today. The question is not simply how accurately machines remember us. It is what kinds of change their representations allow.

Archives can preserve dignity, recover neglected histories, and make institutions answerable for past actions. They can also expose private lives or give a partial account the authority of permanence. Their value depends on how records are contextualized, who can interpret them, and how people represented within them are treated.

For Artificially Intelligent, the archive is therefore both a subject and a method. The Data Stream brings historical records into conversation with emerging technology, while keeping the difference between evidence and interpretation visible. Its essays are situated attempts to understand a changing world. Sources allow a reader to question those attempts rather than simply inherit them.

The clay tablet and the digital trace are not equivalent technologies. Their distance helps us see a recurring problem: human beings create systems to make life legible, then later understand life through what those systems retained. What began as an instrument becomes an inheritance.

We are all archives now because traces of our lives increasingly circulate beyond the circumstances that gave them meaning. Yet the archive remains a record of a life, not its replacement. The responsibility ahead is to preserve what helps us understand one another without allowing what has been recorded to become the limit of who we can be.
