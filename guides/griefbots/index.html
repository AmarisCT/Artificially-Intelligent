Griefbots: what to know about AI versions of people who have died

Some companies now offer chatbots and video avatars built from a person's messages, voice and photos, so the people they leave behind can keep talking to them. Here is what the research says so far, what it doesn't, and what to think about before you try one, or before someone makes one of you.

If you're grieving right now, take this at your own pace. Nothing here says you should or shouldn't use one. If grief feels like too much to carry, a doctor or a grief counselor can help.

What a griefbot is

Our readingA griefbot is an AI system set up to talk like a particular person who has died. Researchers also call them deadbots, postmortem avatars or generative ghosts. Most are built by feeding a chatbot what the person left behind: text messages, emails, voice notes, video. Some answer in text; newer ones speak in the person's voice or appear as a moving video.

ReportedHow little it can take is part of the controversy. In November 2025 the app 2wai advertised video avatars built from as little as three minutes of footage. Its ad showed a woman asking an avatar of her late mother for advice, then the avatar reading bedtime stories to her baby. Many viewers called it “nightmare fuel,” and legal experts raised questions about consent and who owns the data.[4]

What the research says so far

The studies are recent, small and short-term. They describe early experiences and expectations, not long-term effects.

People found them comforting, even when the bot got facts wrong

Reported Manning and colleagues, DIS 2026 conference · 16 volunteers who wanted to talk with an AI version of someone they had lost[2]

Most found the conversations positive and often comforting. When the bot invented details, such as a job the person never had, many carried on. Mistakes in tone bothered them more, like a nickname the person would never have used. Several worried about relying on it too much.

Worth knowing: a researcher checked every reply before participants saw it. Apps you can download don't do that.

“Neither inherently beneficial nor inherently harmful”

From the source Lu and colleagues, Frontiers in Digital Health, June 2026 · interviews with 25 adults in Guangdong, China[3]

People valued the idea of comfort, a continued bond, and a way to say things left unsaid. They also worried about griefbots interfering with natural grieving, giving a false picture of the person, creating dependency, and misusing personal data. The authors found that comfort and risk come from the same thing: the bot's ability to seem like the person is still present.

Worth knowing: participants were describing what they expected, not experiences from long-term use, and all came from one region.

What ethicists warn about

From the sourceIn 2024, Cambridge researchers Tomasz Hollanek and Katarzyna Nowaczyk-Basińska described three situations that could go wrong:[1]

  • A bot made without consent that starts advertising in a grandmother's voice once a free trial ends, with no way to switch it off.
  • A bot left for a child by a dying parent, which starts giving confusing answers, such as suggesting they meet in person.
  • A long subscription that keeps messaging family members after death, which no one can cancel without breaking the contract.

They recommend asking for consent from people before they die, age limits, clear reminders that you are talking to an AI, no advertising in the person's voice, and dignified ways to retire a bot, even a kind of digital funeral. They also say the rights of the people who talk to a bot matter, not only the person it imitates.[1]

What the law covers

ReportedIn December 2025, New York enacted a law requiring consent from the rights holders before the name, voice or likeness of certain people who have died is used for commercial purposes. It covers people whose identity had commercial value, such as public figures, and performers, including AI “digital replicas” of performers.[5]

Our readingLaws like this are mostly about famous people and commercial use. A private family griefbot made from your own messages is, in most places, a legal gray area: what usually decides what happens to the data is the app's terms of service. This is not legal advice, so check the rules where you live.

Questions to ask before you try one

Our reading Drawn from the research and recommendations above.

  • Would they have wanted this? Did the person agree, and would the rest of the family be comfortable with it?
  • Who owns what you upload? Read what the company may do with the messages, voice and video you give it.
  • Can you switch it off for good? Look for a clear way to delete the bot and all of its data, and check what happens if you stop paying.
  • Does it sell anything? Avoid services that advertise or upsell in the person's voice.
  • Does it always say it's an AI? Clear reminders protect you on hard days.
  • Are children involved? Researchers suggest age limits, and children may not understand what they are talking to.
  • How will you know when to stop? Decide in advance what a good ending would look like, and keep the people in your life close.

Recording your own wishes

Whatever you decide about AI versions of yourself, these settings decide who can reach your accounts and data after you die.

Google

From the sourceInactive Account Manager lets you pick up to 10 people to contact, and what data each can download, if your account goes unused for a period you choose. Without a plan, Google may delete an account inactive for two years.[6]

Apple

From the sourceSettings → your name → Sign-In & Security → Legacy Contact. Your contact needs the access key you share and a death certificate. They can reach photos, messages, notes, files and backups, but not passwords or purchases.[7]

Facebook

From the sourceChoose a legacy contact to look after your memorialized profile, or ask for your account to be permanently deleted after death. A legacy contact can't log in as you or read your messages.[8]

In writing

Our readingWrite down whether you would want an AI version of yourself, what it may be built from, and who gets to decide. Keep it with your will or the letter you leave for family.

An open question

SpeculationA griefbot is made from a person's records, the kind of archive we wrote about in We Are All Archives Now. It can echo how someone wrote, but it is built from what they happened to leave behind. Is it a memory of the person, a portrait of them, or something new wearing their voice? The research can't settle that yet. Our Identity dossier keeps the question open.
